What New Jersey requires, and when

Transition planning in ABA means teaching the specific skills that adult life will ask for, starting while there is still time to teach them. Self-advocacy. Daily living routines. Managing unstructured time. Communicating needs to someone who does not already know your child. We work on these in your home and in the community where your child will actually use them, because a skill that only appears in a therapy room is not a skill your child gets to keep.

New Jersey starts earlier than federal law does. IDEA requires transition services in the IEP by age 16. New Jersey regulation requires transition planning to be addressed in the IEP in place for the school year in which your child turns 14, or earlier if the IEP team decides it is appropriate (N.J.A.C. 6A:14-3.7(e)11). Your child must be invited to that meeting, and to every IEP meeting after it. Age-appropriate transition assessments follow at 16.

That is the school side. The adult services side runs on a completely different clock, and the gap between the two is what catches families out.

Until age 21, developmental disability services in New Jersey run through the Children's System of Care, administered by PerformCare under the Department of Children and Families. From 21 onward, they run through the Division of Developmental Disabilities under the Department of Human Services. These are separate agencies with separate applications and separate eligibility determinations.

Here is the part worth writing down. Your child can be evaluated by DDD for functional eligibility starting at 18, but DDD services do not begin until 21, and require Medicaid eligibility. Eligibility for the Children's System of Care ends the day before the 21st birthday. DDD begins offering transition-assistance from age 16.

So the sequence that actually works looks like this: transition planning enters the IEP at 14, DDD transition assistance becomes available at 16, the DDD eligibility application goes in at 18, and DDD services start at 21 on the day the children's system stops. Families who wait until 20 to start the application are often still waiting when school-based services end.

Special education itself continues until your child graduates with a diploma or ages out at 21. The IEP ends on that day. Whatever your child can do independently by then is what they carry into a system that will not be organised around them the way school was.

That is the case for starting skill-building early, and it is why the age 14 requirement exists rather than being a formality.

How we work with teens in New Jersey

Everything we do happens in your home and in the places your child already goes. For a six-year-old that is mostly the living room. For a sixteen-year-old it is much less useful to stay there, so sessions move toward the settings where adult skills get used: the kitchen, the bus stop, the store, the routine of getting somewhere on time and handling it when something changes.

Programmes are designed and supervised by a BCBA and delivered by Behavior Technicians who work with your child consistently rather than rotating through. For a teenager who has been through years of services and staff turnover, that consistency is not a nicety. It is often the thing that determines whether the work goes anywhere.

Assessment takes about four weeks, and there is no onboarding waitlist, which matters more in the transition years than at any other point. A family whose child is 19 does not have six months to spend on a waiting list.

Your BCBA can contribute to the IEP process.
