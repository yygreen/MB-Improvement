What the DAN protocol was

Defeat Autism Now was a project of the Autism Research Institute, launched in the mid-1990s. It trained and listed practitioners, known as "DAN doctors", who treated autism as a biomedical illness caused by toxins, immune dysfunction, and nutritional problems. The protocol typically combined:

  • Supplement regimens, including high-dose vitamins, minerals, and other nutraceuticals
  • Restrictive diets, most commonly gluten-free and casein-free
  • "Detoxification", most notably chelation therapy, which uses drugs designed to strip heavy metals from the body

The underlying theory was that autism is a form of poisoning, usually blamed on mercury, and that removing the "toxins" could remove the autism.

What the evidence says

That theory has been tested, and it did not hold up.

The most rigorous evaluation is a Cochrane systematic review of chelation for autism (James et al., 2015). Cochrane reviews are the standard reference for weighing medical evidence, and this one is blunt. Across the entire published literature the authors found only one trial sound enough to include, and it had methodological limitations. Their conclusion: "no clinical trial evidence was found to suggest that pharmaceutical chelation is an effective intervention for ASD."

They went further than "unproven". Citing prior reports of serious adverse events, specifically hypocalcaemia, kidney impairment and reported death, the review concluded that the risks of using chelation for autism currently outweigh proven benefits. That is an unusually strong statement for a Cochrane review, and it is the sentence to carry away from this page.

The dietary arm has not fared better. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of six randomized trials of the gluten-free, casein-free diet found no significant effect on clinician-rated core autism symptoms, and flagged possible gastrointestinal side effects. We look at that evidence, and at why reviews of it reach different headlines, on our GFCF diet page.

The picture on supplements is more nuanced, and worth stating accurately rather than conveniently. A 2019 meta-analysis in Pediatrics pooled 27 double-blind randomized trials covering 1,028 people with autism. It concluded that the evidence "does not support nonspecific dietary interventions as treatment of ASD", while suggesting "a potential role for some specific dietary interventions in the management of some symptoms". Omega-3 and certain vitamins showed measurable but modest effects on particular symptoms, rather than on autism itself.

That distinction matters. Some targeted supplements may help with specific difficulties, under medical supervision. That is a long way from the DAN premise, which was that autism is a poisoning to be flushed out.

Why "Defeat Autism Now" ended

The Autism Research Institute discontinued the DAN practitioner registry in 2011. The scientific case had not come together, and the safety record had become impossible to ignore.

The name is retired, but the ideas did not vanish. They resurface under new labels: "biomedical treatment", "autism recovery", "detox protocols". The packaging changes; the evidence has not.

Red flags worth remembering

The DAN era taught families some durable lessons. Be cautious with any provider or program that:

  1. Claims autism is caused by toxins, vaccines, or "poisoning". Decades of research, including a meta-analysis covering more than 1.2 million children, have found no such link.
  2. Sells both the testing and the treatment. DAN practices often billed for lab panels that mainstream laboratories consider uninformative, then sold the supplements those panels "indicated".
  3. Promises recovery or cure. Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference. Real therapies build skills and quality of life; they do not remove autism.
  4. Asks you to distrust every mainstream doctor. Good practitioners welcome second opinions and publish their evidence.

What actually helps

None of this means nothing can be done. The opposite. The interventions with real evidence behind them are less dramatic than a detox protocol, and considerably more useful:

  • Behavioral therapy, delivered with clear goals and progress data
  • Speech and occupational therapy matched to your child's needs
  • Treating real co-occurring conditions, such as sleep problems, GI issues, anxiety and epilepsy, with real medicine
  • Parent training, which reduces family stress and supports child progress

If your child has genuine nutritional gaps or GI symptoms, those deserve evaluation by a pediatrician or gastroenterologist, as health problems in their own right rather than as "autism toxins". FAQ

Is the DAN protocol still available?

The official registry closed in 2011, but practitioners offering similar "biomedical" regimens still exist. The evidence against the protocol's core claims has only grown since.

Is chelation ever legitimate?

Yes, for confirmed heavy-metal poisoning, diagnosed with validated testing. Using it to treat autism is not supported by any sound trial, and the Cochrane review concluded its risks outweigh its proven benefits.

Are supplements a waste of time, then?

Not necessarily, but they are not an autism treatment. The best current evidence points to modest effects from specific supplements on specific symptoms, not on autism itself. That is a conversation to have with your child's doctor, based on your child's actual bloodwork and symptoms.

My child improved on a DAN-style diet. Was it the diet?

Children develop, therapies accumulate, and restrictive diets rarely start in isolation. Blinded trials exist precisely because individual stories, however sincere, cannot separate cause from coincidence. When researchers removed the guesswork, the effect on core symptoms stopped being detectable.
