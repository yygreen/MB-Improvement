# Vitamin D and Autism: What the Evidence Supports, and What It Doesn't

Vitamin D is one of the few autism topics where the research is genuinely interesting and genuinely unresolved at the same time. That combination makes it easy to overstate in either direction, so this article tries to separate three questions that often get merged into one.

1. Do autistic children tend to have lower vitamin D levels? **Fairly consistently, yes.**
2. Does that mean low vitamin D helps cause autism? **Unproven, and the association alone cannot answer it.**
3. Does taking vitamin D treat or prevent autism? **The evidence does not currently support that claim.**

Most of the confusion in this area comes from treating an answer to question 1 as if it settled questions 2 and 3.

## What the association research shows

A meta-analysis of case-control studies has found that children and adolescents with autism have significantly lower circulating vitamin D concentrations than comparison groups. Prospective work has also found lower levels in children later diagnosed, measured during gestation and in early childhood.

That is a real and repeatedly observed finding. It is also, on its own, unable to tell you which way the arrow points. At least three explanations fit the same data equally well:

- Low vitamin D contributes in some way to autism risk.
- Autism leads to lower vitamin D, because autistic children more often have restricted diets, spend less time outdoors, or have sensory reasons to avoid sun exposure.
- Something else influences both.

Observational studies cannot distinguish between these. Only trials can.

## Why the supplementation claims do not hold up

This is where a lot of published material, including the earlier version of this page, went wrong.

The most widely circulated claim is that vitamin D during pregnancy cut autism recurrence in high-risk families "from 20% to 5%". That figure comes from a single study by Stubbs and colleagues, published in 2016. Its actual design:

- **19 women.** That is the whole sample.
- **Open label.** Everyone knew who was taking what.
- **No control group and no blinding.** The 20% comparator was a historical figure from previous literature, not a group of women followed alongside.
- Published in ***Medical Hypotheses***, a journal specifically for speculative proposals rather than settled findings.

One of 19 children was diagnosed. With that sample size, a single case either way moves the headline percentage by more than five points. The study's own authors described it as preliminary and uncontrolled.

The other commonly cited evidence is "two open-label trials showed improvement in about 75% of children". Open label means no placebo and no blinding, in a population where expectation effects are well documented and symptoms fluctuate naturally. Those trials justify running a proper randomised study. They do not justify a recommendation.

## The dosing problem

The previous version of this article listed specific doses: 5,000 IU per day during pregnancy and 1,000 IU per day in infancy and early childhood.

We have removed them, and the reason matters. **The tolerable upper intake level for vitamin D in adults, including during pregnancy, is 4,000 IU per day.** The figure previously published here sat above it. For infants under six months the upper level is 1,000 IU per day, so the infant figure sat right at the ceiling.

Those numbers came from the 19-person study described above. Publishing them on a therapy provider's website, next to a claim about reducing autism, came close to functioning as dosing advice. That is not our role, and it was not safe.

Vitamin D dosing during pregnancy or infancy is a conversation with an obstetrician or paediatrician, ideally informed by an actual blood level rather than a number from an article.

## What is reasonable to conclude

Vitamin D deficiency is worth identifying and correcting because vitamin D deficiency is bad for bones, immune function, and general health. That is reason enough, and it applies to every child.

Autistic children may be at higher risk of deficiency for practical reasons: selective eating is common, and so is limited time outdoors. Asking your paediatrician whether a level should be checked is a sensible question.

What the current evidence does not support is treating vitamin D as an autism intervention, or as something that prevents autism. If a well-designed randomised trial changes that, we will update this page.

## What the causes actually look like

For context on where autism does come from: the largest study ever conducted, [Bai et al. 2019 in *JAMA Psychiatry*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31314057/), followed **2,001,631 children across five countries** and attributed roughly **80% of autism liability to inherited genetics**, with maternal and shared-environment effects at 0.4% to 1.6%. Whatever role nutrition plays, it is operating in a small remainder, not the main story.

## FAQ

**Should I have my child's vitamin D level checked?**
It is a reasonable question for your paediatrician, particularly if your child eats a narrow range of foods or spends little time outside. Treat it as general health, not autism treatment.

**Is vitamin D supplementation harmful?**
Within recommended limits it is routine. Above them it is not benign, which is why doses belong with your doctor rather than in an article.

**Does vitamin D during pregnancy prevent autism?**
Current evidence does not support that. The study most often cited for it enrolled 19 women, had no control group, and was published in a hypothesis journal.
