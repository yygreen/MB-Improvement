## The draft was wrong, and checking the citations caught it

The banked draft led with "when the randomised trials are pooled, the GFCF diet
does not improve core autism symptoms" and titled itself "What Six Trials
Found". Verifying the three agreed PubMed additions before writing showed that
framing to be one-sided:

- Quan et al. 2022, Nutrition Reviews (34617108): a GFCF diet "can reduce
  stereotypical behaviors and improve the cognition of children with ASD";
  benefits "promising". POSITIVE.
- Yu et al. 2022, Frontiers in Neurology (35359629): 7 RCTs, 338 participants,
  SMD -0.51 (95% CI -0.81 to -0.21) on core symptoms. POSITIVE and significant.
- Shi et al. 2026, umbrella review (41865680): all dietary interventions
  pooled, overall SMD -0.26 (-0.33 to -0.18). Small, significant, not
  GFCF-specific.

Against:
- Keller et al. 2021, Nutrients (33573238): 6 RCTs / 143 children, clinician-
  reported core symptoms SMD -0.31 (-0.89 to 0.27), no effect; parent-reported
  functioning MD 0.61 (-5.92 to 7.14), no effect; GI adverse effects RR 2.33
  (0.69 to 7.90); GRADE low to very low.
- Fraguas et al. 2019, Pediatrics (31586029): 27 DOUBLE-BLIND RCTs, n=1,028,
  "does not support nonspecific dietary interventions as treatment of ASD".
- Sathe et al. 2017, Pediatrics (28562286): insufficient evidence.

The pattern is not noise: the reviews reporting benefit rest on largely
single-blind trials (Quan says so explicitly), while the reviews reporting no
benefit either restrict to double-blind trials (Fraguas) or use clinician-rated
outcomes (Keller). So the article's subject became the disagreement itself and
what explains it, which is both more accurate and more useful to a parent than
either camp's headline. Title changed to "GFCF Diet and Autism: Why the Studies
Disagree".

This is the second time this week that verifying before writing changed an
article's conclusion rather than just its sourcing. Worth keeping as the
default order of operations.
