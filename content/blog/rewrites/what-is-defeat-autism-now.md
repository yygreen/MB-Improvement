# What Was "Defeat Autism Now"? The DAN Protocol, Explained

If you have been researching autism treatments late at night, you may have run into the phrase "Defeat Autism Now", or seen a practitioner described as a "DAN doctor". The name sounds hopeful, decisive even. So it is worth being direct about what this protocol actually was, why it no longer officially exists, and what the research found when its claims were finally put to the test.

Mastermind Behavior is a BCBA-owned ABA therapy provider. We spend our days with autistic children and their families, and we know exactly how it feels to want to do *something more*. That is why we think families deserve a straight answer on this one, because the DAN protocol's story is, at its heart, a story about what happens when hope gets ahead of evidence.

## What the DAN protocol was

Defeat Autism Now (DAN) was a project of the Autism Research Institute, launched in the mid-1990s. It trained and listed practitioners, known as "DAN doctors", who treated autism as a biomedical illness caused by toxins, immune dysfunction, and nutritional problems. The protocol typically combined:

- **Supplement regimens**, including high-dose vitamins, minerals, and other nutraceuticals
- **Restrictive diets**, most commonly gluten-free and casein-free
- **"Detoxification"**, most notably chelation therapy, which uses drugs designed to strip heavy metals from the body

The underlying theory was that autism is a form of poisoning, often blamed on mercury, and that removing the "toxins" could remove the autism.

## What the evidence says

That theory has been tested, and it failed.

The most rigorous evaluation is a [Cochrane systematic review of chelation for autism](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26114777/) (James et al., 2015). Cochrane reviews are the gold standard for weighing medical evidence, and this one is unambiguous: across the entire published literature, only one trial was sound enough to include, and it had methodological limitations. The review found **no clinical trial evidence that chelation is an effective intervention for autism.**

The review's conclusion went further than "unproven". Because chelation carries documented, serious risks, including dangerously low blood calcium, kidney injury, and reported death, the authors concluded that **the risks of using chelation for autism currently outweigh proven benefits.**

The dietary arms of the protocol have not fared better. A [2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of six randomised trials of the gluten-free/casein-free diet](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu13020470) (Keller et al., *Nutrients*) found no effect on clinician-reported core autism symptoms, and flagged possible gastrointestinal side effects.

The picture on supplements is more nuanced, and worth stating accurately rather than conveniently. A [2019 meta-analysis in *Pediatrics*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31586029/) (Fraguas et al.) pooled 27 double-blind randomised trials covering 1,028 people with autism. Its conclusion was that the evidence "does not support nonspecific dietary interventions as treatment of ASD", while suggesting "a potential role for some specific dietary interventions in the management of some symptoms". Omega-3 and certain vitamin supplements showed measurable but modest effects, averaging around 0.31 in standardised terms, on particular symptoms rather than on autism itself.

That is a meaningful distinction. Some targeted supplements may help with specific difficulties, under medical supervision. That is a long way from the DAN premise, which was that autism is a poisoning to be flushed out.

## Why "Defeat Autism Now" ended

The Autism Research Institute discontinued the DAN practitioner registry in January 2011. The scientific case had collapsed, and the human cost had become impossible to ignore, including the death of a five-year-old autistic boy during a chelation session in Pennsylvania in 2005.

The name is retired, but the ideas did not vanish. They resurface under new labels: "biomedical treatment", "autism recovery", "detox protocols". The packaging changes; the evidence has not.

## Red flags worth remembering

The DAN era taught families some durable lessons. Be cautious with any provider or programme that:

1. **Claims autism is caused by toxins, vaccines, or "poisoning".** Decades of research, including a [meta-analysis covering more than 1.2 million children](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24814559/), have found no such link.
2. **Sells the treatment and the testing.** DAN practices often billed for lab panels that mainstream labs consider meaningless, then sold the supplements those panels "indicated".
3. **Promises recovery or cure.** Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference. Real therapies improve skills and quality of life; they do not remove autism.
4. **Asks you to distrust every mainstream doctor.** Good clinicians welcome second opinions and publish their evidence.

## What actually helps

None of this means nothing can be done. The opposite. The interventions with real evidence behind them are less dramatic than a detox protocol, and far more useful:

- **Behavioural therapy (ABA)**, delivered with clear goals and progress data
- **Speech and occupational therapy** matched to your child's needs
- **Treating real co-occurring conditions**, such as sleep problems, GI issues, anxiety and epilepsy, with real medicine
- **Parent training**, which meta-analyses consistently show reduces family stress and improves child outcomes

If your child has genuine nutritional gaps or GI symptoms, those deserve evaluation by a paediatrician or GI specialist, as health problems in their own right rather than as "autism toxins".

## FAQ

**Is the DAN protocol still available?**
The official registry was shut down in 2011, but practitioners offering similar "biomedical" regimens still exist. The evidence against the protocol's core claims has only grown since.

**Is chelation ever legitimate?**
Yes, for confirmed heavy-metal poisoning, diagnosed with validated tests. Using it to treat autism is not supported by any sound trial and has caused serious harm.

**Are supplements a waste of time, then?**
Not necessarily, but they are not an autism treatment. The best current evidence points to modest effects from specific supplements on specific symptoms, not on autism itself. That is a conversation to have with your child's doctor, based on your child's actual bloodwork and symptoms.

**My child improved on a DAN-style diet. Was it the diet?**
Children develop, therapies accumulate, and restrictive diets often coincide with other changes. Blinded trials exist precisely because individual stories, however sincere, cannot separate cause from coincidence. When researchers removed the guesswork, the core-symptom effects disappeared.
